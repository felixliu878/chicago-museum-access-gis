PPHA 38520 GIS Final Project

Mapping Cultural Access Gaps in Chicago

A GIS analysis of museums, galleries, and arts centers as neighborhood cultural infrastructure.

Felix Liu, Qi Liu, Zhiyuan Zhou

May 2026

We open with the framing: Chicago is culturally rich, but the question is whether that richness is reachable from every neighborhood. The deck is about spatial access, not a complete measure of attendance or cultural belonging.
Research Question

Does Chicago's cultural geography produce equitable neighborhood access?

Our hypothesis: cultural institutions cluster around central, lakefront, and tourist-oriented areas, leaving longer access times in parts of the South, West, Northwest, and Southwest Sides.

  • We test whether access gaps align with income, poverty, race and ethnicity, and transportation disadvantage.
  • We compare two definitions: museum-only access and broader cultural-institution access.
  • We treat access as a spatial equity screen, not as a full measure of cultural participation.

Data And Method

We connect cultural locations to residential geography.

1

Build inventory

OpenStreetMap museums, galleries, and arts centers inside Chicago; CTA L stops provide transit context.

2

Attach neighborhoods

2024 ACS tract attributes and official community areas make the access patterns demographically and locally legible.

3

Measure proximity

Tract representative-point distance to the nearest institution, converted to walking-time proxy at 3 mph.

4

Compare equity

QGIS maps, population-weighted group summaries, museum-only sensitivity test, and a descriptive tract model.

The measure is intentionally modest: it captures spatial opportunity, not total lived accessibility.

Finding 1

Cultural institutions are not evenly distributed.

0.70 nearest-neighbor ratio for broad cultural institutions

Ratio = observed mean nearest-neighbor distance divided by the expected distance under a random point pattern.

  • Clusters appear around the Loop, Near North Side, Near West Side, Lincoln Park, Hyde Park, and lakefront corridors.
  • Large portions of the outer city have few nearby cultural points even before we consider museum-only access.
Source: QGIS map from OSM, City of Chicago, CTA, and ACS-linked geography.
Use the map visually. Point out the dense east-central cluster, the CTA rail context, and the thinner outer geographies. The nearest-neighbor ratio is observed mean nearest-neighbor distance divided by expected nearest-neighbor distance for a random point pattern with the same number of institutions over the broad-institution convex-hull area. Here observed is about 555 meters and expected is about 791 meters, so 0.70 means institutions are closer together than random.
Finding 2

Broad cultural access is strongest near central and lakefront areas.

18.8 population-weighted average walking minutes to the nearest broad institution
  • Shorter access concentrates around the Loop, North Side, Hyde Park, and adjacent cultural corridors.
  • Longer access appears in far Northwest, Southwest, West, and far South or Southeast communities.
Time proxy = representative-point straight-line distance in miles / 3 mph x 60.
This is the main access map. Avoid overclaiming: the map is a proximity proxy, not a transit or street-network travel time. The calculation is distance from each tract representative point to the nearest broad cultural institution, divided by 3 miles per hour and multiplied by 60. The big story is centrality plus edge-neighborhood exclusion, not a clean North-South split.
Finding 3

Poverty matters, but it does not explain the geography by itself.

High poverty 20.9 min
Low poverty 17.8 min
  • Hue shows poverty tercile; shade shows access time, with darker shades meaning longer access.
  • Some high-poverty tracts near central cultural clusters still have short proximity.
  • Some lower-poverty outer neighborhoods have long access because they sit far from institution clusters.
Bivariate colors: poverty determines hue; access time determines light-to-dark shade.
Explain the bivariate map with one sentence: each poverty group has its own color family, and darker shades mean longer access. The most concerning locations are high-poverty and long-access together. Then pivot: poverty alone is not the strongest raw divide in the project.

This slide should land the equity claim. Population-weighted means treat each resident equally rather than each tract equally: multiply each tract value by its ACS population, sum those products, and divide by total population in the group. Use the within-one-mile shares to make the gap concrete.

Galleries and arts centers soften the geography, but they do not erase the spatial equity gap.

The point is not that galleries and arts centers are substitutes for museums. The point is that our answer changes by institution definition, which is why the paper tests both.
Descriptive Model

After controls, centrality, density, and race/ethnicity still structure access.

Population-weighted tract model

Interpretation in plain English

  • Distance from the Loop is the strongest predictor: about 2.1 more walking minutes per additional mile.
  • Population density predicts shorter access.
  • Black and Hispanic population shares remain positively associated with longer access times.
  • Poverty and no-vehicle rates are not statistically distinguishable from zero in the full model.
Population-weighted WLS; HC1 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors; n = 763 complete tracts.
Keep the caveat in the same breath as the finding. Regression flow: start with 782 Chicago tracts; drop records missing model variables, mostly missing median income, leaving 763 complete tracts; outcome is broad_walk_min; predictors are poverty rate, no-vehicle household rate, Black share, Hispanic share, log median income, density per 10,000 residents per square mile, and distance to the Loop; weights are ACS tract population; estimation is weighted least squares; standard errors use HC1, a heteroskedasticity-robust correction. Poverty is borderline at p = 0.051 and no-vehicle is p = 0.213 in the full model, so we should not treat either as a clear independent positive predictor after controls. It still supports the bigger point: the gap is geographic and racialized, not just economic.
Conclusion

Chicago's issue is not cultural abundance. It is uneven cultural proximity.

What the maps imply

Cultural equity should be measured as neighborhood infrastructure, not only as audience outreach or citywide institutional prestige.

Support for neighborhood-based cultural organizations matters most in lower-access areas on the outer South, West, Northwest, and Southwest Sides.

What future work should add

Replace straight-line proximity with GTFS-based transit time, then add hours, cost, disability access, language access, programming relevance, and safety.

Validate the OSM inventory against local cultural directories so smaller organizations are not missed.

Bottom line: abundance and equity are different spatial questions.
End with the key sentence: abundance is not the same as equitable spatial access. This gives a practical policy interpretation while staying honest about the study's limits.
